# Workout Science — Citation Vetting & Copy Review

**Purpose:** Apple rejected MoodRx under Guideline 1.4.1 (health info must carry citations). The supplement screens already have citations; this covers the **18 workout `why` blurbs**, which make health claims with no sources.

**Method:** Each claim was verified against the actual paper (bibliographic details confirmed via Crossref/DOI; supporting line quoted by a research agent). Nothing was cited from memory. Where a claim was overstated, mis-attributed, or unsupported, it was **flagged and rewritten** rather than force-cited — you approved "propose softened copy" + "anchor citations for load-bearing claims."

**⚠️ Bigger finding than expected:** most blurbs weren't just missing citations — they contained inaccurate science (invented percentages, a backwards "exercise burns off cortisol" mechanism, mis-dated/mis-attributed studies, and wellness lore like the psoas "storing stress"). Proposed rewrites below fix all of it while keeping your voice.

**Two items still open (flagged inline):** `foggy-3` needs one dive-reflex citation pinned; `good-2` likely needs no citation after softening. Your call on both.

## ANXIOUS

### anxious-1 — Rhythmic Walking
**Changes:** Removed "bilateral movement reduces amygdala hyperactivity" (that's EMDR research, not walking — blog-sourced only). Removed "default mode network" naming. Fixed the cortisol claim (a single 20-min bout raises cortisol; reductions come from *regular* training).
**Citations to display:**
- Bratman GN, Hamilton JP, Hahn KS, Daily GC, Gross JJ (2015). Nature experience reduces rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activation. *PNAS*, 112(28), 8567–8572.
- Laborde S, Allen MS, Borges U, et al. (2022). Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: A systematic review and a meta-analysis. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 138, 104711.

**Proposed copy:**
> Walking pulls you out of your head. A steady, rhythmic walk — especially outdoors — quiets the brain's rumination circuitry and gives the spiral somewhere else to go. The counting keeps your mind busy with something other than catastrophizing. The 4-7-8 breathing at the end is the real lever: slow breathing with a long exhale measurably boosts vagal tone — your body's brake pedal. Make it a habit and it compounds: regular rhythmic movement helps dial down baseline stress over time.

### anxious-2 — Anxiety-Release Yoga
**Changes:** Single-session effect is real but *small-to-moderate* (SMD ≈0.32) — softened "significant." Removed pose-specific parasympathetic claim for legs-up-the-wall and the "hip flexors store tension" trope (anatomically loose + unsupported).
**Citations to display:**
- Yin J, Tang L, Dishman RK (2021). The effects of a single session of mindful exercise on anxiety: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Mental Health and Physical Activity*, 21, 100403.
- Streeter CC, Whitfield TH, Owen L, et al. (2010). Effects of yoga versus walking on mood, anxiety, and brain GABA levels: A randomized controlled MRS study. *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*, 16(11), 1145–1152.

**Proposed copy:**
> Yoga's mix of controlled breathing and movement nudges your nervous system out of fight-or-flight and toward "rest and digest." A single session reliably takes the edge off anxiety. Restorative postures like legs-up-the-wall let your body downshift; hip openers like pigeon stretch the areas that clench when you're tense. The point is the slow stretch and the breath that comes with it.

### anxious-3 — Steady-State Cardio
**Changes (high-risk line):** Removed "aerobic exercise is as effective as medication for GAD" — that equivalence comes from the *depression* literature (SMILE trial), not GAD; the actual anxiety meta-analysis showed exercise beats *no treatment*, not medication-equivalence. Removed the "threat-detection mode" invented brain-state.
**Citations to display:**
- Streeter CC, Whitfield TH, Owen L, et al. (2010). *J. Altern. Complement. Med.*, 16(11), 1145–1152. *(GABA)*
- Aylett E, Small N, Bower P (2018). Exercise in the treatment of clinical anxiety in general practice – a systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMC Health Services Research*, 18(1), 559.

**Proposed copy:**
> Sustained moderate cardio raises your brain's GABA — the neurotransmitter that tells anxious neurons to quiet down — and floods you with feel-good endorphins. The rhythm is the therapy: steady, repetitive movement gives your churning mind something simple to lock onto, and the rumination loosens its grip. Multiple meta-analyses confirm aerobic exercise is a genuinely effective treatment for anxiety — not a consolation prize.

## LOW

### low-1 — Mood-Boost Strength
**Changes:** Numbers verified exact (33 RCTs, effect size 0.66 ✓). Removed only "comparable to antidepressant medications" — the paper explicitly does *not* make that comparison.
**Citations to display:**
- Gordon BR, McDowell CP, Hallgren M, Meyer JD, Lyons M, Herring MP (2018). Association of Efficacy of Resistance Exercise Training With Depressive Symptoms: Meta-analysis and Meta-regression Analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials. *JAMA Psychiatry*, 75(6), 566–576.

**Proposed copy:**
> Resistance training triggers BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor) — basically Miracle-Gro for the brain. Depression physically shrinks the hippocampus; strength training helps grow it back. Every rep is a small dopamine hit, and compound movements like squats and lunges recruit multiple muscle groups for a bigger neurochemical response. A 2018 meta-analysis in *JAMA Psychiatry* across 33 randomized controlled trials found resistance exercise produced a clinically meaningful reduction in depressive symptoms (effect size 0.66).

### low-2 — Dance It Out
**Changes:** "2019 trial" → it's actually **2020** (Hyvönen et al.). Softened the "dopamine, serotonin AND norepinephrine simultaneously" claim — in humans only dopamine/norepinephrine are confirmed (peripherally), serotonin isn't established for this.
**Citations to display:**
- Hyvönen K, Pylvänäinen P, Muotka J, Lappalainen R (2020). The effects of dance movement therapy in the treatment of depression: A multicenter, randomized controlled trial in Finland. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, 1687.
- Basso JC, Suzuki WA (2017). The effects of acute exercise on mood, cognition, neurophysiology, and neurochemical pathways: A review. *Brain Plasticity*, 2(2), 127–152.

**Proposed copy:**
> Music and movement together are a combination your brain can't resist. Movement nudges up the same feel-good chemistry — dopamine and norepinephrine — that tends to run low in depression, and music's pull on your brain's dopamine reward system rides along for free. A 2020 randomized trial found that adding dance movement therapy to standard care beat standard care alone for depression — and the edge held at a 3-month follow-up.

### low-3 — Nature Walk ✅ MOSTLY ACCURATE (one fix)
**Changes:** Nature/sgPFC specifics all verified (90-min walk, sgPFC, rumination, urban control). **One fix applied during the consistency check:** the original said the gratitude step "activates the medial prefrontal cortex and **releases dopamine**" — same overstatement flagged in good-3 (the imaging study measured mPFC activation, not dopamine release). Changed to "lights up the medial prefrontal cortex — the region tied to perspective and reward," and added the Fox citation.
**Citations to display:**
- Bratman et al. (2015). Nature experience reduces rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activation. *PNAS*, 112(28), 8567–8572.
- Fox et al. (2015). Neural correlates of gratitude. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 1491.

## FOGGY

### foggy-1 — Brain-Clearing HIIT
**Changes:** Removed "cerebral blood flow +30% within 10 min" (CBF actually plateaus/falls at HIIT intensities), "elevated for hours," and "up to 4 hours" memory (verified only to ~30 min).
**Citations to display:**
- Mou H, Tian S, Fang Q, Qiu F (2022). The immediate and sustained effects of moderate-intensity continuous exercise and high-intensity interval exercise on working memory. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 13, 766679.
- Szuhany KL, Bugatti M, Otto MW (2015). A meta-analytic review of the effects of exercise on brain-derived neurotrophic factor. *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, 60, 56–64.

**Proposed copy:**
> Brain fog tracks with sluggish blood flow and low alertness chemistry. Hard intervals push back on both. Going all-out pumps more oxygen-rich blood toward your brain than sitting still ever will, and norepinephrine — your brain's alertness chemical — surges when you push. A single hard session also bumps BDNF, and research shows it sharpens working memory and processing speed for up to about half an hour afterward.

### foggy-2 — Focus Shadowboxing
**Changes:** "improves executive function in multiple clinical populations" → the verified meta-analysis is in *older adults*; boxing RCTs (Parkinson's) mainly show motor/balance gains. Softened to the general complex-movement→EF link.
**Citations to display:**
- Contreras-Osorio F, Ramirez-Campillo R, Cerda-Vega E, et al. (2022). Effects of Sport-Based Exercise Interventions on Executive Function in Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 19(19), 12573.

**Proposed copy:**
> Throwing combinations forces your brain to plan and your body to execute at the same time — more for your head to chew on than plodding cardio. Coordination-heavy, complex movement is exactly the kind exercise research links to sharper executive function and attention. And swinging it out just feels good when your head's a fog.

### foggy-3 — Cold + Movement ✅ RESOLVED (option A)
**Changes:** Big mechanism fix. The dive reflex is *vagal/parasympathetic* (calming, lowers heart rate) — not an "adrenaline/alertness" trigger. The 530% noradrenaline figure is from **whole-body** cold immersion (Šrámek 2000), NOT cold water on the face — so we can't cite it for a face splash. Reframed as a vagal reset / pattern-interrupt.
**Citations to display:**
- Richer R, Zenkner J, Küderle A, Rohleder N, Eskofier BM (2022). Vagus activation by Cold Face Test reduces acute psychosocial stress responses. *Scientific Reports*, 12(1), 19270. *(directly supports the cold-face → vagus → stress-reduction framing)*

**Proposed copy:**
> Cold water on the face flips the dive reflex — your vagus nerve kicks in and your system resets. It's a hard pattern-interrupt for a foggy head. Then you move, and the contrast snaps you into the moment. Think of it as rebooting a frozen computer.

## RESTLESS

> **Theme:** all three were built on a backwards mechanism — that intense exercise "metabolizes / depletes / discharges" stress hormones. It *raises* them acutely; the calm is the post-exercise comedown + a small, real anxiety drop. All three rewritten honestly.

### restless-1 — Explosive Energy Burn
**Citations to display:**
- Connor M, Hargreaves EA, Scanlon OK, Harrison OK (2023). The Effect of Acute Exercise on State Anxiety: A Systematic Review. *Sports*, 11(8), 145.

**Proposed copy:**
> Restlessness is energy with nowhere to go — your nervous system revved, no outlet. Explosive moves like jump squats and burpees give it somewhere to land. You won't "burn off" stress hormones on command — intense exercise actually spikes them first — but hard effort followed by recovery reliably takes the edge off: even a single bout of exercise produces a small, real drop in anxiety. Go hard, then let the comedown do its work.

### restless-2 — Heavy Bag / Pillow
**Changes:** "psychological release" / catharsis is contested — Bushman (2002) showed venting *increases* anger. "More efficient than non-contact exercise" is unsupported (the positive boxing studies were mostly non-contact). Kept the exercise benefit, dropped the catharsis mechanism.
**Citations to display:**
- Bozdarov J, Jones BDM, Daskalakis ZJ, Husain MI (2022). Boxing as an Intervention in Mental Health: A Scoping Review. *American Journal of Lifestyle Medicine*, 17(4), 589–600.
- Bushman BJ (2002). Does Venting Anger Feed or Extinguish the Flame? Catharsis, Rumination, Distraction, Anger, and Aggressive Responding. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 28(6), 724–731.

**Proposed copy:**
> Hitting something hard feels good, and the exercise behind it is what helps: studies on boxing-style training report meaningful drops in anxiety and depression symptoms — mostly from the workout, not the "venting." Worth knowing: research on anger finds that wailing on something to vent tends to *stoke* anger, not drain it. So don't ruminate — just move. Throw combos, breathe with the rounds, and let the effort settle your system.

### restless-3 — Sprint Intervals
**Changes:** Removed "fastest known method," "complete sympathetic discharge," "calming effect that lasts 2-4 hours," "six intervals," and "enforced calm" — all unsupported/invented specificity.
**Citations to display:**
- Connor M, Hargreaves EA, Scanlon OK, Harrison OK (2023). The Effect of Acute Exercise on State Anxiety: A Systematic Review. *Sports*, 11(8), 145.

**Proposed copy:**
> When you feel like you'll crawl out of your skin, max-effort sprints give that energy a job. You won't "metabolize" stress hormones on the spot — intense exercise actually spikes them first — but the recovery after each sprint is where your body downshifts, and a single hard session reliably nudges anxiety down. Empty the tank, then walk it off and feel the comedown.

## STRESSED

### stressed-1 — Stress-Melt Sequence
**Changes:** Removed "reduce physiological arousal by 30-40%" (no source for the number). Softened "metabolizes stress hormones."
**Citations to display:**
- Toussaint L, Nguyen QA, Roettger C, et al. (2021). Effectiveness of Progressive Muscle Relaxation, Deep Breathing, and Guided Imagery in Promoting Psychological and Physiological States of Relaxation. *Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine*, 2021, 5924040.
- Laborde S, Allen MS, Borges U, et al. (2022). Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: A systematic review and a meta-analysis. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 138, 104711.

**Proposed copy:**
> This sequence pairs hard effort (the circuit) with a deliberate wind-down (the stretching and breathing). The circuit burns off nervous energy; the stretch-and-breathe phase flips you into recovery mode. Progressive muscle relaxation is a clinical technique shown to dial down physical tension and arousal. And the 4-7-8 breathing is the lever — slow breathing with a long exhale measurably boosts vagal tone, your body's brake pedal.

### stressed-2 — Yin Yoga
**Changes:** Removed "stress tightens fascia, yin reverses it at the tissue level" and the entire "psoas stores stress / contracts during fight-or-flight" claim — both are wellness lore with zero peer-reviewed support (highest debunk risk). Softened DBT link to mindfulness.
**Citations to display:**
- Hylander F, Johansson M, Daukantaitė D, Ruggeri K (2017). Yin yoga and mindfulness: a five week randomized controlled study evaluating the effects of the YOMI program on stress and worry. *Anxiety, Stress, & Coping*, 30(4), 365–378.

**Proposed copy:**
> Yin yoga's long, passive holds give tight areas time to release and let your nervous system settle — a five-week randomized trial found the approach significantly reduced stress and worry. The long holds (3–5 minutes) are uncomfortable by design: staying with mild discomfort is a kind of practice in tolerating it — the same muscle mindfulness training builds. The hip openers target where a lot of us hold tension.

### stressed-3 — Rhythmic Cardio
**Changes:** Removed "synchronizes brainwave activity" (no evidence for exercise), "reduces cortisol" (acute bouts raise it short-term), and "reduces perceived stress by 20-30%... persist for 4-6 hours" (invented numbers).
**Citations to display:**
- Ensari I, Greenlee TA, Motl RW, Petruzzello SJ (2015). Meta-analysis of acute exercise effects on state anxiety: an update of randomized controlled trials over the past 25 years. *Depression and Anxiety*, 32(8), 624–634.

**Proposed copy:**
> Repetitive rhythmic movement — rowing, cycling, swimming, brisk walking — is one of the oldest, most reliable ways to settle a stressed system. The counting task narrows your focus and interrupts the stress thought-loops. Twenty minutes of steady rhythmic cardio reliably takes the edge off: acute exercise measurably lowers anxiety. Make it regular and the stress-hormone benefits build over time.

## GOOD

### good-1 — Personal Best
**Changes:** Removed "good mood pre-elevates BDNF → stronger neuroplasticity" and "personal bests set persistent neuromuscular baselines" — both unsupported framing. Kept the verified acute-BDNF fact.
**Citations to display:**
- Szuhany KL, Bugatti M, Otto MW (2015). A meta-analytic review of the effects of exercise on brain-derived neurotrophic factor. *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, 60, 56–64.

**Proposed copy:**
> When you feel good, training feels easier — so use it. A single hard session reliably bumps BDNF, the growth factor your brain uses to adapt. Chase a personal best while the tank's full. The gains you bank today are still there on the days you'd rather stay in bed.

### good-2 — Something New ✅ RESOLVED (citation-free)
**Changes:** Removed "neurogenesis at rates higher than repetitive exercise" — primary study (Fabel 2009, mice) shows the *opposite*: running drives neuron production, novelty aids survival, they're additive. Removed "extraordinarily efficient," "strongest predictor of retention," and the mis-applied hippocampal-consolidation mechanism. After softening, the copy makes **no specific physiological claim** — so it may need **no citation**.
**Citations to display:** *(likely none — decision below)*

**Proposed copy:**
> Learning a new movement while you're in a good mood is a smart play. Novelty challenges your brain in ways repeating the same routine can't, and a positive state makes the awkward beginner phase easier to sit with. New skills build new coordination. Lean into being bad at something today — then save it for a bad day.

### good-3 — Gratitude Walk
**Changes:** Removed "releases dopamine and serotonin" (the neuroimaging study measured mPFC activation, not neurotransmitters) and the "stronger than either alone" synergy claim (never studied head-to-head). Kept the two verified facts.
**Citations to display:**
- Fox GR, Kaplan J, Damasio H, Damasio A (2015). Neural correlates of gratitude. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 1491.
- Cregg DR, Cheavens JS (2021). Gratitude Interventions: Effective Self-help? A Meta-analysis of the Impact on Symptoms of Depression and Anxiety. *Journal of Happiness Studies*, 22(1), 413–445.

**Proposed copy:**
> Pair an easy walk with a little gratitude. Both have real, independent track records: walking lifts mood, and gratitude practice gives a small but reliable boost to how you feel. Gratitude also lights up the brain's medial prefrontal cortex — the region tied to perspective and reward. Stack them and the walk becomes something you can come back to on harder days.
